The 60-second picture

Everything here exists to answer one question over and over: “Is this betting market priced wrong, and by enough to beat the fees?” When the answer is yes, the system places a (pretend) bet and later checks whether it was right.

What the whole thing is

A “pod shop” is a collection of small, independent trading strategies (each one is a pod) that all run inside one engine. Each pod hunts for a specific kind of mispricing — a tennis favorite that's too cheap, a golfer under-priced to finish top-10, a live game where the crowd overreacts. They all share the same plumbing: the same way to size a bet, the same risk limits, the same trade ledger, the same scoreboard.

The system runs on a rented Linux server (a DigitalOcean “VPS” / “droplet”) and wakes up every 5 minutes to scan the markets. It is in paper mode — it records what it would bet and tracks the results, but never risks real money. The goal is to prove each strategy actually works before any real capital is considered.

The seven jobs, in plain words

Get the data Decide fair value Run the strategies Control the risk Orchestrate the loop Settle the results Watch over it all
  • Get the data — clients that fetch live prices & scores from Kalshi, Polymarket, sportsbooks (The Odds API), Deribit, MLB, and golf/econ feeds.
  • Decide fair value — the math that turns messy market odds into a single honest probability (remove the bookmaker's cut, blend sources, model win probability).
  • Run the strategies — the pods themselves; each compares fair value to the market price and flags a bet when the gap clears the fees.
  • Control the risk — position sizing (Kelly), portfolio exposure caps, and a central ledger so no single pod can blow the budget.
  • Orchestrate the loop — the engine that wires it all together and runs the 5-minute cycle.
  • Settle the results — after games finish, look up who won and score every open bet.
  • Watch over it all — a fund-manager layer that checks the system is healthy and emails you a daily brief.

Why the order matters. The rule is paper-first, CLV-gated: prove the edge on historical data and lock the kill-criteria before writing the pod, so a losing idea can't be quietly rationalized into staying alive. This came from a real lesson — a strategy (P-013) lost $2,094 of paper money while its success criteria were still being argued after the fact.

The agent layer — how an idea gets judged

Everything above this point is deterministic: code that scans, prices, and settles the same way every time. This section is the part that exercises judgement — and the whole design question is how to let a language model propose strategies without letting it promote its own work.

The short version

Twelve specialist agents, each with a narrow job and no ability to do the next one's. A scout can propose but not specify. A reviewer can verify but not validate. A validator can measure but not deploy. Nothing reaches real money without a named human approval — and that isn't a policy note, it's a line of Python that raises an exception.

The agents don't run themselves. A background worker records what they produce; a person (or a Claude session) actually invokes them. A running service does not mean research is happening — that distinction has its own alarm, because it's the easiest thing in the system to get wrong.

The strategy factory — six roles, one direction of travel

An idea moves left to right. Each stage produces one typed JSON artifact, and the next stage can't start until the previous one exists and passes.

The states an idea can be in

A strategy sits in exactly one state. The arrows below are the only legal moves — anything else raises StrategyTransitionError rather than being politely ignored.

Two of those steps are hard-blocked in code, not by convention:

  • You cannot validate before integrity passes. Ask, and it raises.
  • You cannot promote into live_small or live_scaled without an approval_ref — a human's name against the decision. Agents are told never to invent one, and the dataclass refuses to construct without it either way.

How work actually moves — the inbox is the authority

Agents don't talk to the registry. They write a JSON file into a role-specific inbox, and a small worker picks it up every 60 seconds. The clever part is what grants permission: authority comes from which folder the file landed in, never from anything the file says about itself. A spec dropped into the monitoring inbox is rejected, no matter what it claims to be.

What it has actually done

The factory went live on 2 Aug 2026. Its first real subject was a fantasy-football dead-heat idea, which it killed at the integrity gate: the market family turned out to be season-long single-rank contracts, not the weekly threshold markets the thesis assumed. The idea had a plausible mechanism and no product to trade it on.

It was then run over P-022 — a strategy already validated by hand — purely to check whether the agents could be trusted. That run found a real defect nobody had noticed: the backtest harness reports a pooled average where the locked decision rule specifies an equal-weighted-per-tournament one. On the original sample the two agree, so the original green light stands. On the widened sample they don't: +2.6¢ becomes +1.5¢ and stops being statistically significant.

Read that as the intended outcome. A factory whose first two runs both ended in "no" is behaving correctly; the failure mode to fear is the one that says yes to everything.

Research intake — where ideas come from

The factory above judges ideas. This is the part that finds them — and its guiding principle is that the existence of a market, a paper, or a popular post is an input, never evidence that an edge exists.

Sources in, ranked assignments out

Deterministic code owns everything mechanical: fetching, hashing, de-duplicating, recording provenance, checking whether a venue may even be researched. Agents are used only where judgement is genuinely required — and they sit at the far right, after the cheap filters have already run.

The wall between finding and doing

This is the most important structural fact in the whole research system, and it is deliberately boring: nothing discovered here can walk itself into the trading registry.

  • Research assignments carry may_enter_strategy_registry: false — permanently, as a field.
  • No census or intake output matches the shape the strategy queue accepts, so a seed cannot be mistaken for a strategy request even by accident.
  • Whether a venue may be researched, and whether a new idea may call itself executable, is a separate registry with a named human approver and an expiry date.
  • A surviving idea becomes an OpportunityCard, which an authorised person must then hand to the factory. That handoff is manual on purpose.

The Texas venue classifications in that registry are operational controls, not legal advice.

The timers do not invoke an agent. They fill an inbox. A person or a Claude session has to actually do the research — so a green timer is evidence that tasks were created, never that anything was reviewed. Only a stored disposition proves review happened.

How effort gets allocated

Assignments are ranked by what they're worth against what they cost to check:

P(real edge) × capacity × expected half-life × executability
─────────────────────────────────────────────
research time + data cost

The deterministic ranker can measure provenance, eligibility, similarity to past work, and attention decay. It cannot know real edge or capacity — so those stay null until a specialist produces evidence. Penalties apply for prior rejection, missing timestamp-correct data, rule ambiguity, unrealistic fills, fees, and crowding.

research-director is the agent that works this ranking — it allocates effort across venues and source lanes, orders the queue by what a check is worth against what it costs, and holds the exploration floor open. Its job is triage, not judgement about any single idea.

A 15–20% exploration floor is held even once metrics accumulate, so a small early sample can't permanently starve a new source or an unfamiliar market family.

The pod roster — strategies & their gates

Each pod is one betting strategy. A gate is the pre-registered amount of evidence it must gather in paper mode before anyone considers real money. No pod has cleared its gate — everything is still validating.

PodStrategy — the edge in one lineStatusGate to clear

How to read a pod's status

  • active validating — running live on paper, gathering evidence toward its gate. Do not scale it up until the gate is met.
  • shelved — code exists and may work, but it's parked. P-002/P-006 are shelved because they'd have to execute on Polymarket, which this project has no access to — so they can never graduate regardless of results.
  • retired / killed — failed its gate and was shut off, with a post-mortem. P-016's live-maker edge was −1.29¢ markout (a loss after fees); P-017M's promising number turned out to be a weighting-bug artifact.
Why gates are locked in advance. The fund manager reads the gate rule literally — “NO DECISION at n<120” means no decision, no matter how good the numbers look mid-sample. This is the guardrail against fooling yourself with a lucky early streak.

The north-star metric: CLV, not P&L

Pods are judged on CLV (closing-line value) versus the de-vigged sharp close — i.e. did you get a better price than where the smartest book eventually settled — not on raw paper profit. Over small samples, P&L is mostly luck; beating the closing line is the durable signal that an edge is real. Every edge must also clear the bet net of fees + half the spread before it counts.

Safety gates — why no real money can slip out

  • Demo environment — Kalshi keys are demo-only; KALSHI_ENVIRONMENT=demo.
  • No live flag — order placement stays gated unless an explicit I_UNDERSTAND_LIVE_TRADING flag is set (it isn't).
  • Global invariant — the monitor treats any pod reporting a non-paper mode as a P1 critical alert.
  • Risk caps — 25% Kelly sizing, 50% max portfolio exposure, 5% daily-loss cooldown, on a $1,000 paper bankroll.

Fund manager — the layer that watches for you

You shouldn't have to stare at a dashboard. The fund manager answers three questions every day: Is production healthy? What's waiting on me? How close is each strategy to its decision point?

The design rules (they're the whole point)

  • The registry is the source of truth. registry.yaml is the one place that says what every workstream is, its gate, and what it's blocked on — replacing state that used to be scattered across 9 memory files, 6 research dirs, a stale status doc, and two crontabs.
  • Alerting never touches an AI. Threshold checks are plain Python — a model in the alert path can fail in ways that look like silence. The AI is for judgment on top of facts already collected.
  • Report by exception. A strategy that just needs more calendar time stays silent until its gate fires. A daily report that repeats unchanged gets skimmed — and then it protects nothing.
  • Exit code 0 ≠ it worked. Every scheduled job is judged on output freshness, never on whether it exited cleanly. (A settlement job once ran “successfully” while writing zero rows for 31 hours.)
  • Never write to the trading system. The whole manager is read-only against data, configs, and logs — it can't cause an incident.
